About Me

Jonas Noguera

Hi, I am Jonas Noguera
and I used to be
that type of scientist

With paper notebooks on the bench
pipettes in my hands
and gel electrophoresis photos on my desk

If you could travel back in time and take a peak into one of those notebooks, you would have probably found some method to encapsulate a cell-free expression system; or if you could take a look at those pictures, you would have seen, probably, the glowing band of a newly created DNA construct. This is because our lab, back in the day, was trying to build a synthetic cell using a bottom up approach. Or in other words, we just used to toss a bunch of biochemicals in a beaker, shake them, and hope the whole thing would assemble into some nucleotide, protein-filled, life-ressembling lipidic vesicle.
Easy.

As that type of scientist, my life was simple and predictible: from the office to the lab with a new protocol to try out; and then once again back into my office with exciting results waving in my hands. All while whisteling and singing christmas songs in the middle of the summer. I was happy.

But then something happened ...

Do you remember those little life-ressembling lipidic vesicles? Suddently I needed to count them out by hand over confocal microscopy images. Thousands of vesicles, hundreds of pictures. You do the math. I can assure you that at that moment, I was not singing anymore, something that my lab mates were probably really thankful for. There had to be a way to avoid long, long hours in front of a computer screen in a dark-lit, empty office, way past the go-home time for the day.

The computational way

Somehow, I got convinced that I was going to train a machine learning, computer vision model known as a HAAS cascade to first detect and then count those vesicles. To top it off, my plan included the coding and implementation of a graphical user interface for the continuous training of the model. Of course with absolutely no previous experience.
Easy.

I had a lot of ground to cover.

First step? Mastering Python and machine learning from the absolute zero.

In my mind, this first step was the bitter, hard pill I had to swallow in pursue of the higher, data-shaped reward.

Little did I know that it was precisely the journey, rather than the destination, what started to beat my heart.

I loved it so much, so fast, that everyday after work I dedicate four, five or more hours just looking into YouTube tutorials, then articles, then documentation.

I swallowed so much content that I even downloaded an app on my phone to practice my new skills while waiting at the station, on the tram, in the train ... I was head-to-toes, full body in.

I was on fire

This all culminated in a HASS cascade model attached to a graphical user interface that automatically counted lipid vesicles on confocal images. I achieved a 30 times increase in the processing speed of the images compared to my old, boring visual inspection.

I was happy again.

All this made me think about all those type of scientists. The ones with paper notebooks on their benches, pipettes in their hands and gel electrophoresis photos on their desks. It made me think about scientists in need of a computational way but who don't have the time nor the skills to develop.

It is for this reason that, after the completion of my PhD in 2020, I have dedicated myself to help those type of scientist.
